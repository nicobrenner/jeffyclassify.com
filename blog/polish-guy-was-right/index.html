The Polish guy was right

Oct 2026 · Multilingual · Nico Brenner

Earlier this month a site called openai-hub published an article about Jeffy. It explained, with some confidence, that Jeffy runs on "fine-tuned Qwen3.5 and Gemma 4 models"

Jeffy runs logistic regression

The article was written by an AI, which is funny on its own, given that the entire pitch here is that you should stop using an AI for this. But the mistake isn't the interesting part. The assumption underneath it is: that if something classifies text well, there must be a frontier model inside. That assumption is wrong, and somebody is paying for it

A comment I couldn't stop thinking about

On a Show HN thread in early October, a commenter described building a personal assistant in Polish. He needed to sort incoming messages into categories. He'd tried the available tools

Every one of them returned "none" for every Polish message

Not wrong answers. No answers at all. The tools didn't disagree with him about what the message meant, they declined to have an opinion

Why Polish gets "none"

Frontier labs optimize for English, and they're not being careless about it. English is where the benchmarks are, where the papers are, where the eval sets are, and where most of the people building the models live. The frontier is in English, so that's where the work goes. Other languages arrive later, or much later

If model quality came mostly from model size, that would just be a waiting game. Sit tight, and in two years Polish gets good

For classifiers, the size axis collapses

Quality comes from two things: the data and the model's size. For a classifier, size stops mattering almost immediately. You freeze an encoder, put logistic regression on top, and feed it about 50 examples per class. The resulting head is 90 to 180 KB and trains in under a minute on a laptop CPU

So the bottleneck isn't the model. It's 50 good examples

Which is an afternoon's work for somebody who actually speaks the language, and effectively impossible for a lab that doesn't. "We need better Polish NLP" turns into a data-entry problem, and data entry is the one thing a native speaker does better than a hundred GPUs

The receipts

We ran this on all 51 languages of MASSIVE, 60 intent classes each. Frozen multilingual embeddings, one small logistic regression head per language, no fine-tuning, no GPU

83.3% Mean, 51 languages
1.8pp Behind fine-tuned XLM-R
50/50 Languages beating zero-shot
180KB Per-language head

Fine-tuned XLM-R Base gets 85.1%. We get 83.3%. That is a gap of 1.8 points, in exchange for giving up the GPU, the fine-tuning pipeline, and the week

The more useful comparison is against zero-shot cross-lingual transfer: train XLM-R on English only, then point it at the other 50 languages. That is the technical version of "wait for the frontier to get here." A small head trained on the target language's own data beat it in all 50 non-English languages, by 12.6 points on average, and the advantage was largest for the languages furthest from English

To be straight about it: our heads get labels in the target language and the zero-shot baseline doesn't. That's not me hiding the catch, that is the argument. Those labels cost an afternoon

Where I had this wrong

Until this week I'd have told you there's a limit to this argument, because the encoder still has to be good, and good means big, so we never really escape the frontier

Then I looked at our own encoder sweep. Same pipeline, same data, four different encoders:

Two things in there, and the second one changed my mind

First: mE5-small and MiniLM-L12 have the same parameter count, 118M, and five points of mean accuracy between them. On Swahili the gap is fifteen points, 0.793 against 0.639. Size isn't what separates them. What they were trained on is

Second, and this is the one: the encoder that wins on English is the 22M English-only MiniLM-L6. It beats every multilingual encoder in the table on English, including ones six times its size, and then falls off a cliff on Arabic and Korean at 0.591

Multilingual encoders pay a tax for their breadth. An encoder that only has to speak one language spends all of its capacity on that language

Which points at the next experiment

If a 22M English-only encoder beats a 135M multilingual one on English, then a small Polish-only encoder plausibly beats a big multilingual one on Polish. Same architecture, its own vocabulary and tokenizer, a tiny fraction of the data

That's a hypothesis. I haven't run it, and I'm not going to pretend the table above is the result. But it's why I think it's worth running, and it inverts the usual plan: the way to serve 51 languages well might not be one enormous model that speaks all of them badly

One more piece of honesty while I'm here. The 51 language heads live on the playground right now run on MiniLM-L12, which is the weakest multilingual encoder in that table. LaBSE is what produced the 83.3%. Re-training the live heads is on the list

So: write 50 examples

That's the actual point of this post

Pick your own categories, write about 50 examples per class in your language, and you get a working classifier that runs on your CPU in under a minute. No GPU, no API key, no per-call cost, and your text never leaves your machine. Then it goes in the catalog so the next person who speaks your language starts where you finished

If you speak a language that gets "none" today, you are the only person who can fix that, and it is going to take you an afternoon

The 51 language heads are live at playground.jeffyclassify.com, the code is on GitHub, and the full 51-language write-up with all the figures is in the repo

And to the commenter who told me every tool returned "none" for his Polish messages: you were right, and the offer stands
